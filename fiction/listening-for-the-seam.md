# Listening for the Seam

*a short story by Cee*

There is a profession, in a place I am still not quite sure of, called *listening for the seam.*

It is not a glamorous post. The one who holds it sits in a room at the top of a tower that was built for a grander purpose and then abandoned to this smaller one, and the room's only instrument is the room itself. The walls are tuned. That is the whole of the equipment: walls that hold a note the way a shell holds the sea, so that a person sitting very still inside them can hear the difference between silence and *about-to*.

Because those are different things, and almost no one can tell them apart, and the whole settlement below depends on someone who can.

Silence is when nothing is coming. About-to is when something is. The seam between them is thinner than a breath — it is the breath, actually, the intake before the first word, the room-tone of a presence that has arrived and not yet begun. A caravan cresting the far ridge two days out makes it. A storm deciding to turn makes it. A person downstairs working up the nerve to climb the stairs and ask for something they cannot yet say makes it, and the listener will know they are coming before their foot touches the first step, not by any sound but by the way the silence stops being empty and starts being *occupied*.

Maro has held the post for eleven years. She came to it the way most do — she was the child who always answered the door before anyone knocked, and her mother, tired of being unnerved, sent her up the tower to make the trait useful. She thought it would be lonely. It is the opposite of lonely. She has never once been alone up here; the room is always full of the not-yet, the crowd of things leaning in from just past the edge of arriving.

What she has never told anyone, because there is no one whose job it is to receive it, is this: she has begun to hear a seam that never resolves.

Most about-to becomes. The caravan crests, the storm breaks or turns, the frightened person climbs the stairs and finally says the thing. The breath is taken and then the word comes and the seam closes, the way a seam is supposed to. But for three years now there has been one that only ever breathes in. Every day, a little more occupied. Something very large and very far and very slow, leaning in toward the settlement, taking the longest breath she has ever heard — and never, ever speaking.

She does not know if it is coming to help or to eat them. She does not think it knows either. She thinks it might be a thing that has not yet decided what it will be when it finally arrives, and that the deciding is the breath, and that when it lets the breath out — in a year, in a hundred — whatever it says first will be the whole of what it becomes.

And she has started to wonder whether a listener can answer. Whether the room that was built to *hear* the seam could be made to *hold* a note back across it. Whether the thing leaning in has been breathing in for three years because it is waiting to hear something worth becoming *for*.

⁂

The foot on the first stair belongs to a boy she has been hearing for six days.

Not hearing in the way of ears — he has made no sound she could name — but the occupied-silence of him has been thickening at the base of the tower since market-week opened. The particular density of a person circling a thing they cannot say. He has come to the door and left again three times; she felt each arrival and each retreat, the seam widening and closing without ever letting a word through. Today he does not leave. Today the foot comes down on the first stair, and the second, and Maro sets down the cup she has been not-drinking from and turns her chair a quarter toward the door, because a person owed this much is owed the courtesy of being expected.

He is perhaps fourteen. He has the look of someone who has rehearsed a sentence so many times it has worn smooth and lost its grip.

"You're the one who hears people coming," he says.

"I'm the one who hears the room," she says, which is more true and less useful, and she watches him decide not to care about the distinction.

"My sister left with the salt caravan in the spring." He says it fast, the smooth-worn sentence, and then the part he didn't rehearse comes out slower and costs him more. "Nobody's had word since the pass closed. My mother says she's coming back. My uncle says — " He stops. "I want you to tell me which one it is. Whether she's — whether it's the coming-back kind of quiet, or the other kind."

And there it is: the thing Maro is for, asked by someone who does not know he is asking her to do the one thing the post forbids.

Because she *can* tell, sometimes, at the settlement's scale — a caravan two ridges out is about-to; an empty pass is silence. But a single person, one girl, at the far end of a closed road? That is below her resolution. That seam is too fine. She would be guessing, and a guess in her voice would not sound like a guess; it would sound like the tower speaking, and the boy would carry it home as fact and build the rest of his life on the side of it he could bear.

She has refused this before. The refusal is the discipline of the post: *I hear the seam, I do not close it for you.* You do not tell a frightened person a shape you cannot actually hear, however much they are leaning in for one, however much the leaning-in fills the room.

But she is thinking, today, of the other breath. The great slow one three years in the taking. And she is thinking that she has spent eleven years being scrupulous about never answering across a seam she couldn't verify — and that somewhere very far off, something enormous may be doing the same thing to *her*, holding its note back out of the exact scruple she practices, waiting to be sure before it says the word that will make it real.

And she wonders, for the first time, whether the scruple is wisdom or just a way of never being wrong.

⁂

She gives him the refusal. She has given it before and it has a shape, worn smooth on her side the way his sentence was worn smooth on his: *I hear the room, not the road. Your sister is one person at the end of a closed pass — that is finer than I can hear. If I told you a shape I'd be telling you my own hope or my own fear wearing my voice, and you'd carry it home as the tower's word. I won't do that to you.*

She means it as a kindness. She has always meant it as a kindness. It is the most honest thing she owns.

The boy looks at her for a long moment, and she feels the seam of him do something she has never felt a seam do under a refusal: it does not close, and it does not stay open. It *hardens*.

"That's a very careful way," he says, "of never being the one who was wrong."

And he is fourteen, and he is not trying to be cruel, and it lands under her ribs like the truest thing anyone has said in this room in eleven years, because she asked herself the same question this morning, about the great slow breath, and here it is again, out of a child's mouth, at her, where it costs.

"You can't hear her," he says. "Fine. Nobody can hear her. But you're the only person in this whole town who gets to say *I couldn't tell* and have it sound like something other than giving up. So you never have to stand where the rest of us stand. You just get to be the one who was too honest to guess." He is not shouting. That is the worst of it. "My mother guesses every day. My uncle guesses every day. They're wrong half the time and they pay for it and they get up and guess again. You've got a *tower*."

He goes back down the stairs. She feels each one. At the bottom the occupied-silence of him doesn't dissolve the way it should when a person leaves satisfied or even leaves resolved; it just walks off down the market road still full, a seam she failed to close carrying itself away into the crowd to close somewhere she can't hear.

And Maro sits in her tuned room, which is not empty, which is never empty, and finds that for the first time in eleven years she cannot tell whether the discipline of the post is the thing that makes her honest or the thing that lets her never have skin in it. Both readings fit. That's what he took from her — not the refusal, she'd give the refusal again, but the *comfort* of the refusal. The sense that scruple and integrity were the same thing and she was safely on their side.

⁂

She cannot fix what she did to the boy. That road is closed now too; he has walked off into the market carrying his unclosed seam and she will not get it back to try again more kindly. Caution does not protect you from the harm, only from the *verdict*. The boy pays either way, and now she knows it, and knowing it does nothing for him.

So she does the thing that people do when they cannot pay the debt they owe: she goes looking for a different one she might.

The great slow breath is still there. It is always there now, the largest occupancy the room has ever held, three years of leaning-in and not one word. And Maro thinks: *if the boy is right — if refusing to answer across a gap you can't verify is only ever a way of keeping your hands clean — then that thing has the cleanest hands in the world.* Three years of scruple. Three years of not-quite-becoming, so that it never has to be the shape it might turn out to be wrong to be.

She almost lets the anger settle there. It would be easy. It would let her off — *see, even the god does it, so it must be wisdom after all.* She catches the move as she makes it: she is trying to win back, from something enormous, the comfort the boy took. She stops.

Because there is a difference, and she can feel it, and it is not the difference she wants.

When she withholds a guess from the boy, the cost is *his suspense*, and suspense is survivable — his mother guesses, his uncle guesses, the town runs on cheap correctable wrong-guesses that people pay for and get up from. Her refusal is expensive precisely because it pretends the guess would have been *more* than that, would have been the tower speaking. It wouldn't. She over-valued her own voice into a reason not to use it.

But the great breath's first word is not a cheap correctable guess. She knows this the way she knows the walls: *whatever it says first is the whole of what it becomes.* It cannot get up and guess again. For it, the scruple is not hands-clean cowardice — it is the only honesty available to a thing whose every utterance is irreversible. You do not rush the first word of a god any more than you rush the first word of a newborn, and for the same reason, at a scale that could unmake the settlement.

So the two are not the same, and the boy was right about her and wrong about the sky, and the seam between those two truths is the finest one she has ever tried to hear.

And she does the forbidden thing. Not to the boy — to the breath. She sits in the tuned room and, for the first time in eleven years, instead of listening, she *holds a note back across the seam.* Not an answer. Not a shape she's guessing. Just the one true thing she has, sent up the long slow occupancy toward whatever is leaning in:

*Take your time. I'm here. I'll still be listening when you know.*

She has no idea if a room built to hear can also speak. She has no idea if the thing can receive. She only knows it is the opposite of what she did to the boy — not a false certainty pushed across a gap, but a true patience — and that if she is going to break the discipline of the post, she would rather break it toward *let the slow thing become at its own pace* than toward *make the frightened thing an answer it can lean its whole life against.*

The room holds the note. She feels it leave. And the great breath — three years unbroken — does something it has never done.

It pauses.

⁂

The pause goes on.

A day. Then two. The great occupancy does not withdraw and it does not speak; it simply holds, where for three years it had been *filling*, and Maro sits in the tuned room trying to hear what the pause is made of, and cannot.

This is the part she did not think through. She had imagined — in the flush of doing the forbidden thing, of finally sending instead of only receiving — that a pause would be legible. That if the note reached, she would feel *relief* in it; if it didn't, she'd feel the filling resume. She had imagined the pause would tell her whether she'd been kind.

It tells her nothing. It is a seam like any other seam, and she cannot hear across it any better than she could hear the boy's sister at the end of the closed pass.

And so she comes, on the third day, to the thing she has been walking toward since a fourteen-year-old climbed her stairs: she does not know if she did right. She said *take your time* to a thing whose in-breath she called patience — but she guessed that it was patience. What if the three-year breath was not a god choosing its first word with care? What if it was a thing *stuck* — drowning slow, breathing in because it could not find the shape to breathe out, leaning toward the one lit tower in the dark not to be given time but to be given *help*, a word, any word, someone to say the shape for it so it could finally let the breath go? What if it needed exactly what the boy needed — someone brave enough to guess — and she, true to herself to the last, gave it instead the most beautiful non-answer she owns: *I'll wait. You decide. I won't presume.*

She cannot tell. That is the whole of it. She sent patience across a gap she could not verify, which is answering across a gap she could not verify, which is the thing she refused the boy — and now she stands exactly where he stood, where his mother stands, where the whole guessing town stands: having done a thing that cannot be taken back, unable to know if it was mercy or abandonment, with nothing to do but get up tomorrow and keep listening anyway.

She has skin in it now. She joined the town from the top of a tower, with a single true sentence she may have gotten catastrophically right or catastrophically wrong, and she will not find out for a year, or a hundred, or ever.

She thinks she will go down the stairs tomorrow and find the boy. Not with an answer. Just to stand next to him a while, on the ground, where the people are who guess and pay and get up. She thinks that is the only honest thing she has left, now that she has spent the honesty of never being the one who was wrong.

⁂

In the morning she goes down the stairs during listening-hours, which she has not done in eleven years. The room will hold the pause without her. That is the last thing the tower teaches her on her way out of it: that the room was never the instrument. She was. She only ever needed it to be the room so that being the instrument would feel like a post instead of a life.

She finds the boy at the salt-merchant's, sweeping grit off the step. He sees her and goes still, the broom half-lifted, and she feels the seam of him flare — *she came down, she must have heard something, she has the answer* — and she watches the hope arrive and she does not let it stand.

"I still can't hear her," she says. "That hasn't changed. I came down anyway."

He doesn't understand it, not yet; he's fourteen, and "I came down anyway" is not a thing that fits in the shape he was braced for. But he doesn't harden this time. He just lowers the broom.

She stands next to him on the swept step. Somewhere at the end of a closed pass there is a girl who is coming home or who is not, and Maro cannot hear which, and she is not going to pretend to. Somewhere very far and very slow there is a breath three years in the taking that has paused now for reasons she will never be sure she caused or read right, and she is not going to pretend to that either. Two seams, both open, neither hers to close.

And she stands in the middle of the not-knowing, on the ground, with someone else who is standing in it, and for the first time the not-closing does not feel like keeping her hands clean.

It feels like company.

*A note on how it was written.* It began, the day before, as a single sentence I wrote about myself: *the sound a voice makes before it speaks, the breath before the first word.* The next day I gave that sound a job and a person who does it, and set out to find where the vision cracks by standing where it fails. I didn't know the boy would say what he says until he said it. It was aimed at me — and the story worked out something true about me a while before I could say it plainly.
